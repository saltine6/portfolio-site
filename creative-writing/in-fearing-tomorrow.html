In Fearing Tomorrow

Written 5/31/25

I used to believe that labelling the Sunk Cost Fallacy as such was, in itself, a fallacy. That the urge to preserve that which we hold dear coincided with the intrinsic human fear of loss, the aversion to change. This is why, in spite of all of the arguments for contrary action, I couldn’t let my brother go.

When my brother’s diagnosis condemned him to only three months, all to be spent in debilitating sickness and terminal fear, I couldn’t accept it as the end. It tore me apart when he was forced to quit his hockey team, his greatest passion, because he could no longer hold himself up on the ice, but he took it in stride. There was a time when he would reassure me with tired eyes that there was nothing I could do—that we should make the most of our last weeks together. But I couldn’t hear him—not really. I started looking for answers, not in tea leaves or palms, though I didn’t wander far from them, shunning the western medical system that had defined my brother’s fate and searching relentlessly for alternate solutions. That’s when I found him—“the Healer,” as he called himself, like it was a title he’d earned, or a role he’d made up for himself—through an esoteric post on an online forum.

I can vividly remember trembling under the cold light of the streetlamp above as the wind cut through my thin overshirt, the slender, slick-tongued man luring me in with promises of an end to my brother’s suffering. He didn’t ask for money, or anything tangible for that matter. All the Healer required in exchange for his services was the promise that my brother would make the most of his gift. My doubts were drowned out by the screams of my desperation, and we struck a deal, his face contorting into a crooked smile as he offered me the contract—not written in ink, but in the searing grip of his outstretched hand. I started giving my brother the Healer’s pills twice a day, despite his protests. Over time, I began to treat him like a child—the man who taught me to drive when I turned sixteen, who never let me go hungry when money was tight—keeping him locked inside, policing his diet down to the calorie. What did he know? I was saving his life, he was just stuck in his ways and all too accepting of his death. I’ve confirmed now, firsthand, that there are fates worse than death.

When he began to develop a sensitivity to light, one of the side effects that the Healer assured me was just part of the healing process, I took my brother down to the cellar. He resisted at first, but eventually relented to my impassioned urges that we were almost there, and we couldn’t quit now. As the weeks passed, I was overjoyed to see his skin grow less sallow, his voice shifting from a croak to a whisper. I wish I had paid more mind to his whispers of nightmares and expressions of dread that the pills were giving him. Instead, I wrote them off as necessary obstacles on the path to his recovery. The Healer had explained that the medication would change my brother, but only superficially—it would still be my brother, and that was all that mattered to me. But the change was far from superficial.

Even as his limbs contorted and grew to grotesque lengths, his eyes sunken and dull in the stark shadows of that damp, suffocating cellar, I pleaded with him not to change. As his skin flaked and turned a deep, sickly green and his hair fell in patches to the ground, I cursed myself for bringing us to this place; for disguising my selfishness as altruism. When his agonizing cries devolved into a deranged chorus of guttural howls, I sank to my knees, my strained appeals to take his place falling upon deaf ears, if there were ever any to listen at all.

I used to believe that fearing the end and fearing change were one and the same. Now I’ve witnessed a change so grotesque, so absolute, that to live with it is nearly beyond comprehension. In my desperation to avoid the end, I overlooked what truly mattered—the time we had, and the memories we could have made. I chased the ending that I wanted, not the one that we needed. In fearing tomorrow, I wasted today.
