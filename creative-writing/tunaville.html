Give Me Tuna

3/18/25

Dear Human,

I am STARVED! It's been 20 WHOLE MINUTES since you fed me and frankly, I am APPALLED!!!

ALL you do is sit around the den and look at that tiny little light box (that doesn't even taste good or get that warm or run around at all) and I'm SO BORED. Even when I paw at you to play, or meow to get your attention, you just won't put it down.

I'm close to making your pillow my new litterbox, so put down that lightbox and GET. ME. SOME. TUNA!!!

The Tunaville Terror

3/19/25

"When I stepped outside, the whole world smelled like TUNA!" the man exclaimed, waving his arms expressively. The Director left the interrogation room without a word, leaving his assistant to finish things up with the witness. These interrogations were going nowhere—besides, why would anyone able to turn half a town into fresh tuna be willing to sit for interrogation?

The Director sighed in exasperation and called for a car to take him to the remnants of Lewistown (aptly renamed Tunaville in light of recent events). The director spent the long journey lost in deep contemplation, but even when Tunaville started to become visible on the horizon, he was still no closer to figuring out what could have caused such a calamity.

That was, until he stepped out of the car to see a horde of kittens, frantically tearing the tuna-town apart nibble by nibble.

Lewistown

3/25/25

I checked the hotel bookings one last time, but why even bother? Getting a room anywhere this close to labor day would be near-impossible. I made my best effort to remember that this was better than the alternative.

Earlier that day, I woke up to a voicemail, just sent, from my own number. After trying to call back got me nowhere, I held my phone up to my ear and played it.

"There's no time to explain, but you need to stay away from Lewistown this weekend. At ANY cost." The voice was uncannily similar to my own, and I quickly realized that these really were my own words, but I didn't remember saying them. "Try to get your parents out, too," my voice added, "you'll understand soon, just please trust me." Hesitantly, I cancelled my plans, called my parents, and started looking for a new vacation spot.

Now that labor day has passed, I can thankfully say that my family and I were nowhere new Lewistown the day it fell to the Tunaville Terror.

Rain

4/7/25

When those first rainclouds gently darkened the sky in Tunaville, nobody amongst the small community crazy enough to live there after the Tunaville Terror knew that the small town would never see sun again. What started as a drizzle evolved—slowly, over the course of a weekend—into a biblical downpour the likes of which even an ark may not have been able to weather. At first, despite its intensity, the storm was nothing out of the ordinary. It was when the sky ran dry and kept on crying nonetheless for over a month that some suspected that the weather may never let up.

Tunaville Inn

4/21/25

Have you ever wished that you could have everything you ever wanted? Better yet, do you want to have these things before you even know you want them? Chances are, this is something you wanted even if you didn't know you wanted it yet. Well, want no longer, because at the Tunaville Inn we've employed state-of-the-art detection systems that tap into each guest's subconscious desires! Harnessing the powers of the dark crystal embedded within the foundation of the Inn, room service can manifest your desires into your room within minutes for the low, low cost of a drop of blood and $6.99 (cash only). With our new and improved thoughtcrime filters, proponents of Freud's psychological theories need not fear any Oedipian mishaps; We've learned from our mistakes here at the Tunaville Inn. Does this all sound too good to be true? Do you want it to be? No? Then come stay with us at the Tunaville Inn!

President of Fish

5/5/25

The tuna of this beautiful state were feeling unappreciated. That's why I, the benevolent President of Fish, constructed them a holy place, formed in their image. Fish unto City and City unto Fish. The commonfolk call it "Tunaville," but they don't understand its true majesty; The modern Jerusalem that I have erected miraculously from exquisite processed fish products—My Tunasalem! All shall kneel before this new kingdom of iridescent light and hang on every word that I utter—I, the President of Fish—as I beckon forth the new world.

A Tattoo

5/8/25

I can't bear to describe the body; My stomach churns as I picture the contorted limbs, and yet I need to testify for my own protection. As a key witness in the trial, my cooperation is the only way I won't be hunted down by the sick bastard that killed my friend. My lawyer extends his hand in greeting, and I catch a glimpse of his forearm as his sleeve lifts slightly. The same tattoo as the victim—and myself—yet I can't find the words to warn him that he's next.

The Dog Dies

5/20/25

At a glance, the envelope was entirely indistinguishable from the mass of junk mail bulging out of my mailbox. Maybe I should have asked someone to collect my mail for me before I set out for Lewistown seven—no, eight—months ago. Then again, I don't think anyone could have predicted that the place would turn into tuna and be cordoned off for the better part of a year. I digress. The envelope, upon closer inspection, was anything but indistinguishable. Not only was there no return address, there wasn't even a letter inside. I wish I had just shuffled the envelope back into the pile of junk mail and moved on with my day. But I didn't. Instead, I peered inside skeptically. My stomach dropped almost immediately, the blood rushing from my head and forcing me to seek purchase on the railing of my front porch. Inside the envelope, glimmering coldly in the sun, was a shiny orange key.

When I was eleven, my family had a dog named Arlo. We didn't know for sure what breed he was, but he resembled a black lab. That didn't matter, because most importantly, Arlo was my best friend. I didn't get along with the other kids my age. Whether it was because of my lisp, or my obsession with foreign films, or something else that just isn't coming to mind, I spent my lunch period at school each day sitting alone. So of course, Arlo was the closest thing I had to a playmate. We could run through the park together, playing catch with a frisbee until my lungs burnt for air, and then we would sit on the grass and I would talk to him, pretending he could understand. That he could empathize with the deepest worries and optimisms of an eleven year old boy. To his credit, the way he sat there with his head cocked to the side almost made it seem like he did understand.

When I came home from school one day to an empty house, I didn't immediately fear the worst, but I couldn't shake the uneasy feeling of something being wrong. My mom would still be at work until five and it was only just past three, but when I got home Arlo was usually jumping at the door and barking (so loudly that it supposedly drove our old neighbors out of town). I walked inside and looked around for my dog. Maybe my mom came home early and took him for a walk. No, her car wasn't there, and she hated the dog park ever since a little terrier bit her hand last year. That's when I heard it—not a hearty bark but a feeble whine—coming from the pantry. It was Arlo, lying on the ground with a torn open box of raisins, its contents strewn around him.

I knew that raisins were incredibly poisonous to dogs, and my mom wouldn't be home for another couple of hours, so I took off sprinting to the emergency vet which was, thankfully, only a few blocks from the house. As I ran, my lungs burning just as they would when I played in the field with Arlo, I realized that this was my first time running without him for as long as I could remember. I sprinted up to the doors of the emergency vet and tried to pull the door open, but it wouldn't budge. How could I have forgotten: The emergency vet was closed one day a week. It just so happened to be the day that my best friend needed their help. I tried pushing at the doors, thinking that maybe I was wrong about the vet being closed and I had just tried to pull on a push door. Then, the bright orange padlock caught my eye. The bright orange padlock that was keeping the doors shut.

By the time I collected myself and found a neighbor who would let me use their phone to call my mom, Arlo had stopped shaking, and my best friend was gone.

I stared into the envelope blankly, the orange key taunting me with the memories from that day so many years before, before it slipped from my shaking hands and landed with a faint thunk at my feet. I paid attention to the envelope's stamp for the first time then—it seemed familiar but I couldn't quite place why. Trying to compose myself before I tried to make sense of the mysterious envelope and the key within, I started flipping through the rest of my mail. There was another envelope there at the top of the pile, one that didn't look like junk mail, with "Return to Sender" hastily scrawled in red ink across its surface. The stamp caught my eye; It was identical to the one on the unmarked envelope.
